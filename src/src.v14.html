Why routes, not one long journey?

People arrive with different jobs to do: an organiser needs a plan, a leader needs words and commitments, a teacher needs sources. One linear journey through twenty sections would slow most of them down, while three cards left comms teams, frontline staff and community partners without a way in. Six short routes of three steps each cover every user group in the user needs table, keep each route under an hour, and leave the grouped menus for people who want to browse. This is a design judgement that still needs testing with users; tree testing of the routes is in the research plan.

    What is in this hub

    The 2026 theme

    Honouring Our Communities

    This year's national theme, set by Black History Month UK, asks us to recognise the people whose everyday contributions shape, support and sustain Black life across the UK, and to make those contributions visible.

    Organisations taking up the theme are looking beyond the best-known historical figures to the educators, carers, volunteers, health workers, artists, entrepreneurs and community leaders whose work often goes unseen. The theme also holds that Black history is not confined to the past: it is being made now, in our workplaces and neighbourhoods.

    For the public sector this is close to home. Our services are delivered by, and for, these communities. Honouring them means seeing the colleague who runs the staff network after hours, the volunteer who connects residents to services, and the generations whose labour built the institutions we work in.

    Sources: UAL, quoting BHM UK, Nottingham Trent University, University of York.

    Themes over the years

    National themes since 2021

    Black History Month UK began in 1987. National themes are well recorded from 2021; for earlier years no national theme was found while preparing this edition, so none is shown. Wales has run its own programme, with themes such as "Movers, Shakers and Legacy Makers" in 2019. In the United States, where the month is in February, the 2026 theme marked a century of commemoration.

    The visual language

    Why the hub looks like this

    The design draws on West African visual traditions chosen for what they say about community. They are used with their meanings stated, not as decoration.

    Nkonsonkonson

    An Adinkra symbol of the Akan people of Ghana showing chain links. It stands for unity, human relations and the idea that we are linked in life and beyond. It appears on the theme poster.

    Sankofa

    An Adinkra symbol often read as "go back and fetch it": it is right to return to the past to carry forward what we need. It is the emblem of the History section and the learning poster.

    The woven strip

    The bands along each edge are an original pattern inspired by West African strip-weaving, in which narrow strips are sewn together into one cloth. It is not a copy of any named kente design. Many strips, one cloth, is the theme in miniature.

    The living weave

    The opening animation threads weft lines through points that stand for people. Each thread passes over and under the others, the way a community holds together. It stops if your device asks for reduced motion.

    Ethno-linguistic heritage

    Writing systems made in Africa

    Africa's written heritage is older and wider than many people are taught. Some scripts are thousands of years old; others were invented in the last two centuries to write African languages on their own terms.

    Diaspora and long-standing settlement

    Black communities around the world

    African-descended communities live on every inhabited continent. Some were formed by the transatlantic trade in enslaved Africans, some by Indian Ocean and Arab-world trade and enslavement, and some by migration after empire. A fourth group are peoples who were never part of an African diaspora but were classed as "Black" by colonial and racial systems.

    Equal Earth projection, so continents appear at their true relative size (see the real size of Africa). Where labels would overlap, some are left off the map; every place is listed below. Routes are schematic, not shipping lanes. Dotted red lines show forced transport of enslaved people across the Atlantic; dotted blue lines show the Indian Ocean trade, which included enslavement; dashed gold lines show later voluntary migration.

    Geography

    The real size of Africa

    The world and Africa maps in this hub use the Equal Earth projection, which shows every country at its true relative area. On 4 September 2026 the UN General Assembly adopted the "Correct the Map" resolution, sponsored by African Union member states and the Bahamas, which promotes Equal Earth over the Mercator projection used in many classrooms and apps. It passed 164 to 1, with six abstentions, and is not binding.

    Same places, two projections

    Sourced · S68Areas measured from the map data

    What the maps in this hub use

    • World and Africa maps: Equal Earth, an equal-area projection published in 2018, drawn from Natural Earth country outlines.
    • UK history map: a simple projection scaled for Britain's latitude. At country scale the size distortion is small, so it is fine for locating places but not for comparing areas between continents.
    • Pins: placed from latitude and longitude, so they sit where the places really are.
    • Not shown: small island states such as Cabo Verde and Mauritius, which disappear at this scale. They are still pinned where relevant.

    Mercator still has a use: it keeps directions true, which is why it is used for sea and air navigation. The resolution does not challenge that use.

        Handle with care

        Who counts as Black is a historical question

        Self-identification comes first

        Use the names communities use for themselves. Some Aboriginal Australians use "Blak" as a self-chosen identity. Andamanese peoples are the Great Andamanese, Onge and Jarawa, not "Negritos", a colonial label. Siddi and Habshi communities in India are Indian citizens with African heritage.

        Why include peoples "deemed Black"?

        Colonial law and science sorted people into races using skin colour. The label "Black" was applied to peoples across the Pacific, South Asia and Australia with very different histories from the African diaspora. Showing them makes that sorting visible. It does not claim a shared ancestry or identity.

        Mixed and adjacent heritage

        Many people are of mixed African and other heritage, or come from communities with African roots that are rarely recognised, such as Afro-Indian, Afro-Arab, Afro-Turkish and Afro-Latin communities. Public services should let people describe themselves rather than fit a fixed list.

        Maroon communities

        Maroons were people who escaped slavery and built free settlements: in Jamaica, Suriname, Colombia, Mauritius and beyond. Several are now protected heritage sites. Use the "Maroon heritage" tag to find them.

          Who to bring in

          • Your race or ethnic minority staff network, as partners from the start, with time allowed for the work.
          • Your senior race champion or sponsor.

          Do

            Avoid

              Accessibility

              • Caption all video and live online sessions; publish transcripts.
              • Offer British Sign Language interpretation for large events where you can.
              • Give every image and poster alternative text. The posters in this hub include it.
              • Choose step-free venues, share access information in the invitation, and offer a quiet space.
              • Ask about dietary needs when food is served, and label it.
              • Hold events at varied times so part-time, shift and regional staff can join.

              Words

              • Write "Black" with a capital B when referring to people.
              • Be specific: name the group you mean, such as Black Caribbean or Black African, rather than an umbrella term.
              • Government writing guidance advises against the acronyms ‘BAME’ and ‘BME’. See Writing about ethnicity.
              • Read Words matter on race and ethnicity, why Black and African are not the same, and terms to avoid.
              • Use the words people use for themselves, and ask when unsure.
              • See the thesaurus for preferred and non-preferred terms.

              This is general good practice, not legal advice. Check your organisation's policies, the Civil Service Code (or your sector's equivalent) and your data protection team.

              Getting the tone right
              Joy and remembrance, side by side
              
    
              
      
              Celebrate loudly
              Food, music, stories, achievements, jokes and the jollof debate. Black history is full of joy, invention and community, and it deserves a party.
              
      
              Remember gently
              Give slavery, colonial violence, racist killings and the Windrush scandal their own quiet moment. Don't put them next to games or giveaways, and warn people before showing distressing material.
              
      
              Let people opt in
              Some colleagues carry this history personally. Make attendance optional, offer a quiet room and signpost support, such as your employee assistance programme.
              
      
              Follow the lead of those affected
              Ask your staff network and community partners how they want ancestors and loss to be marked. Different communities do this differently.

              Brand and media hub
              The hub's identity
              An original identity for public sector use in 2026. It is not the Black History Month UK logo; credit the national theme to Black History Month UK wherever you use it.
              
    
              
      
              The mark: three linked rings
              Gold, red and green rings, linked like the Nkonsonkonson chain: people joined in community. Keep clear space of one ring's width around it, and never stretch, rotate or recolour it.
              
      
              Tone of voice
              • Warm and joyful for celebration: food, music, achievement, invitations.
              • Quiet and respectful for remembrance: slavery, racist violence, loss.
              • Plain and specific always: short sentences, real names, dates and sources.
              • Never mix jokes into remembrance, use stereotypes, or speak for a community.

                Method
                The theme was confirmed from several organisations' published Black History Month 2026 pages. Historical items were checked against archives, museums, councils and reference pages where retrieved; the rest are labelled as reference history. All content sits in one data model that drives the pages, posters, graph and export, so a change in one place reaches all of them.
                
      
                Limits
                The official Black History Month UK site itself was not read directly; the theme wording reaches this hub through organisations quoting it. Coverage outside England is still thin, and most sources are in English. Descriptions of cultural motifs and words are summaries that should be checked with community partners. Debates move quickly: facts are dated and reviewed on 21 October 2026. No people's stories are invented.
